# When 01:30 happens twice

Daily learning note | 26 September 2026 | Q005a

Sidiq review status: Not yet reviewed.

## Research question and hypothesis

Can a timezone-aware availability selector still admit information from the future when local clocks
move backwards? The hypothesis is that comparing UTC instants for every timestamp fixes the known
London fold leak while preserving publication, revision, duplicate, latency and staleness rules.

Q002 exposed this defect in Q001 on 22 September. Q004 established that real data access remains
unverified. Repairing the selector is the next independent prerequisite before Q005 ingestion; it
needs no market subscription and makes no empirical investment claim.

## Beginner explanation and terms

A wall clock is a label for time, not a unique position on a timeline. When clocks move backwards,
a label can occur twice. In our London example, the first 01:30 has offset +01:00 and the second
has offset +00:00. A report arriving at the second occurrence was unavailable at the first.

**Instant:** one position on the timeline. **UTC:** the common reference used here for chronology.
**Offset:** the difference between a local clock and UTC. **Named zone:** a location's time rules,
such as Europe/London. **Fold:** the marker distinguishing the earlier and later repeated time.
**Latency:** elapsed processing time after publication. **Age:** elapsed time since observation.

A timezone-aware object contains enough information to locate an instant, but the chosen comparison
operation still matters. Using UTC consistently also gives duplicate detection a common identity.

## Data, metrics and protocol declared before implementation

All observations are invented. The central case uses London 25 October 2026; the regression grid
also uses London 29 March and New York 8 March and 1 November 2026. These are clock-transition
fixtures under installed timezone rules, including future fixture dates, not observed research activity.

Acceptance: reject the one-hour future publication; accept latency at exact equality; reject stale
records despite fresh revisions; select by observation instant then revision instant; distinguish two
fold releases and reject the same release expressed in another zone. Existing UTC results must remain
unchanged. An integer-minute oracle, boundary cases and two identical experiment runs provide checks.
There is no fitted model, tuning search, return series or alpha estimate.

## Mathematics and worked example

Let o, a and d be observation, availability and decision instants expressed in UTC. Let L >= 0 be
processing latency and M >= 0 an optional age limit, in elapsed time units. A valid record has a >= o.
Eligibility requires a <= d and d - a >= L, plus d - o <= M when M is supplied.
For each asset and feature, select the eligible maximum pair (o, a), comparing o first, then a.
A revision changes a and the value; it does not reset o or make an old observation young again.

For this example UTC = local wall time - offset. Conversion must preserve the instant; attaching
a UTC label without changing the clock fields changes what the timestamp means.

| Event on 25 October 2026 | London label and offset | UTC instant |
|---|---|---|
| Earlier decision | 01:30, +01:00, fold=0 | 00:30 |
| Later publication | 01:30, +00:00, fold=1 | 01:30 |

At the earlier decision, d - a = -60 minutes: the later publication must be excluded. The old
raw same-zone predicate instead treated the two local labels as equal and selected it. At the
later decision, a record published at the first 01:30 has waited 60 minutes, so L = 60 passes.
An observation from that first instant has age 60 minutes, so M = 59 rejects it even after revision.

At the spring transition, London 00:30 to 02:30 on 29 March spans only 60 elapsed minutes.
A 90-minute latency must therefore fail, while a 90-minute age limit still passes. Both transitions
matter: local arithmetic can understate or overstate elapsed time.

## Primary sources and design decision

Python's datetime documentation states that comparison ignores fold when operands share the same
tzinfo object, and subtraction in that case does no offset adjustment. Its astimezone operation
preserves the UTC instant. We use that conversion before validation and selection.
https://docs.python.org/3/library/datetime.html

PEP 495 defines the earlier/later fold distinction and explains that constructors permit invalid
local-time instances, including times inside a spring-forward gap. UTC conversion cannot recover
an unspecified source fold or prove that a supplied local clock label existed.
https://peps.python.org/pep-0495/

Python's zoneinfo documentation describes fold-aware conversion and its use of system IANA data
or the tzdata package. The fixture explicitly records offsets so changed rules cause a visible
test failure rather than a silent change in expected chronology.
https://docs.python.org/3/library/zoneinfo.html

These primary sources were consulted on 26 September 2026. Canonical UTC storage is our design
choice, consistent with Q002's label intervals, rather than a claim that all applications require it.

## Limitations and interview explanation

UTC normalization does not verify source clocks, publication truth, calendar sessions, permissions or
historical vintages. It accepts the instant implied by an aware input. Ambiguous strings need an explicit
source policy; nonexistent local times need ingestion validation. Leap seconds are outside this model.
Named-zone tests need an IANA timezone database; no new tzdata dependency was added today.

This changes stored timestamp representation: consumers expecting a London zone label must retain that
metadata upstream or convert explicitly for display. Equal instants now share a canonical duplicate key;
the two distinct fold instants remain distinct. There is no measured speedup or trading-performance claim.

For an interview, draw the two UTC instants first, then show their identical local labels. Explain why
awareness alone did not make the original operations chronological. Walk through the publication and age
inequalities, show a failing test, and distinguish this fix from source-data validation.

## Check your understanding

1. Why can two aware London timestamps showing 01:30 represent different instants?

2. At the second 01:30, does a publication from the first 01:30 satisfy a 60-minute latency?

3. Can a new revision make a 60-minute-old observation pass a 59-minute age limit?

4. Why are equivalent UTC and local representations duplicates, while two fold releases are not?

5. Does UTC normalization prove that an ambiguous timestamp was assigned the correct fold?

## Suggested answers

1. Their offsets differ. In this fixture fold=0 is 00:30 UTC and fold=1 is 01:30 UTC. The local
clock repeats a label while elapsed time continues forwards.

2. Yes. Exactly 60 elapsed minutes have passed and the contract includes equality. A threshold
one microsecond greater would fail, even though local subtraction misleadingly reports zero.

3. No. Age is measured from the original observation instant. A revision updates availability
and value, not the observation's age.

4. Duplicate identity uses asset, feature, observation instant and availability instant. Alternate
zone representations keep those instants unchanged; two folds differ by one hour in this fixture.

5. No. Conversion preserves the instant implied by the supplied input. Choosing the right fold,
rejecting nonexistent local times and verifying source evidence remain ingestion responsibilities.
