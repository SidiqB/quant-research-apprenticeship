# Splits, dividends and the return you actually measure

## Research question and hypothesis

Can an explicit share-and-cash calculation distinguish raw price changes from holding-period returns
across a split and dividend? The hypothesis is that valuing the same opening holding at both endpoints
removes mechanical split effects and counts each dividend entitlement once. Q006 established when
research decisions occur; Q008 now establishes a return arithmetic contract before membership,
features and targets. This is the next eligible non-Wednesday task after C002.

## Beginner explanation and definitions

A share split changes the size of each share. Imagine exchanging one large slice for two smaller
slices of the same cake. Seeing each new slice priced at half the original price does not mean your
holding lost half its value. A dividend also changes what you own: some wealth may move from the
share price into a cash entitlement. We must count both pieces in consistent units.

**Raw price:** unadjusted price per share at that endpoint. **Split ratio s:** ending shares per
opening share, so a 2-for-1 split has s=2. **Reverse split:** consolidation of shares, such as s=0.2
for 1-for-5. **Cash entitlement C:** dividend amount due per opening share. **Capital return:** change
in the value of the shares, excluding cash. **Income return:** dividend entitlement divided by opening
value. **Total holding-period return:** combined change in share value and entitlement over one interval.

**Ex-dividend date:** date governing whether a purchase carries the next dividend entitlement under
the applicable rules. **Payment date:** when the distribution is paid. An entitlement may exist before
cash settles; today's model values that receivable at par and does not make it available for trading.

## Data, metrics and predeclared protocol

All eight examples are synthetic, undated single holding intervals. There is no market-data sample,
fit, security selection, backtest or empirical performance claim. Prices are raw, positive and in one
currency; the holder is assumed entitled to the specified distribution. No within-interval reinvestment.

Before implementation, acceptance required split-only and dividend hand examples, a reverse split,
a combined event exposing unit errors, invalid-input rejection, independent holdings arithmetic and
repeatable output. Metrics are component/total returns, hand-case passes and detected wrong controls.
A 1e-12 absolute/relative comparison tolerance checks finite calculations; it is not a universal accuracy
bound. Unknown action inputs must fail rather than silently become no action. Full project checks
and a verified teaching PDF are required before publication.

## Mathematics from one opening share

Let P0 be the opening price, P1 the closing price, s the ending shares per opening share and C the
cash entitlement per opening share. Opening wealth W0=P0. Closing equity E1=s*P1 and closing wealth
W1=E1+C. Return is the change in wealth divided by opening wealth: R=(s*P1+C)/P0-1.

The raw price return is P1/P0-1. The capital return is s*P1/P0-1. The income contribution is C/P0.
Thus total return equals capital return plus income contribution, up to numeric rounding. For any
positive opening quantity q, both W0 and W1 multiply by q, which cancels in the return ratio.

## Hand-calculated examples

| Scenario | Opening to closing holding | Raw price change | Total return |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary gain | 1*100 to 1*110 | 10% | 10% |
| Ordinary loss | 1*100 to 1*90 | -10% | -10% |
| 2-for-1 split | 1*100 to 2*50 | -50% | 0% |
| 1-for-5 reverse split | 1*20 to 0.2*100 | 400% | 0% |
| Dividend offsets price fall | 1*100 to 1*98 + 2 | -2% | 0% |
| Dividend with gain | 1*100 to 1*103 + 2 | 3% | 5% |
| Split and dividend | 1*100 to 2*49 + 2 | -51% | 0% |
| Split, dividend and gain | 1*100 to 2*54 + 2 | -46% | 10% |

In the combined zero-return example, ending equity is 98 and the entitlement is 2, so total wealth
remains 100. Capital return is -2%, income contributes +2%, and total return is 0%. A dividend quoted
as 1 per ending share must become 2 per opening share before entering this function.

## Three deliberately wrong controls

Ignoring the split in the split-only example reports 50/100-1=-50%. Inverting the split reports
(50/2)/100-1=-75%. Both are false losses caused by inconsistent share units. The correct result is zero.

Multiplying the already normalized cash by the split again gives 2*(49+2)/100-1=+2% in the combined
example. That counts 4 units of cash when the holder is entitled to 2. Passing a post-split quote of
1 directly as C instead creates a -1% result. Parameter names help, but cannot authenticate units.

## Scope of the mathematics

Pure split neutrality is an accounting example, not a forecast that a real price stays fixed through
an event. The examples deliberately choose offsetting prices to expose errors. Actual market movement
can create gains or losses alongside a split or dividend. The reverse-split example permits fractional
shares; real cash-in-lieu arrangements need their own evidence and are outside this calculation.

## Implementation and reproduction

src/quant_research/data/corporate_actions.py adds action_return and an immutable ActionReturn result.
The required keyword inputs are shares_per_old_share and cash_per_old_share. Values 1 and 0 explicitly
assert known absence of an action. Missing arguments, None, strings, booleans, nonfinite values,
nonpositive prices/ratio and negative cash are rejected. Unknown actions are not filled with defaults.

cannot reveal adjusted prices, mixed currencies or incorrect events; those are upstream obligations.

## Limitations and interview explanation

This is one-interval arithmetic, not an event processor or portfolio ledger. It assumes raw prices,
known entitlement and a common currency. Applying a split again to split-adjusted prices, or adding
a dividend to a total-return-adjusted series, can double-count actions. The scalar interface cannot
detect that mistake. Upstream metadata and licensed sample checks remain necessary.

No announcement, ex-date, payment-date or event-order validation is implemented here. Recognizing a
receivable at par does not imply it has settled. Chaining returns with reinvestment requires a separate
policy for timing and financing. Fees, taxes, FX, dividend reinvestment, rights, mergers, spin-offs,
terminal proceeds and fractional-share rounding are excluded. A missing terminal price never justifies
zero recovery; zero endpoint prices are rejected under this narrow scope.

Floating-point rounding remains even when all checks pass. Extreme intermediate values may be rejected
although a rescaled calculation would fit. Small effects can round away; the range guards are not a
complete precision guarantee. The hand examples establish arithmetic correctness, not economic realism.

For an interview, begin with a holding rather than a price chart. State the share basis and dividend
units, derive closing wealth, then distinguish raw price, capital and total return. Explain why unknown
action evidence stays unresolved and why entitlement is different from settled cash. These repository
results are material to study, not a claim that personal understanding has already been demonstrated.

## Check your understanding

1. Why does a 2-for-1 split from 100 to 50 not imply a 50% loss for the holder?

2. After a 2-for-1 split, a dividend is quoted as 1 per new share. What is C?

3. Why is an ex-date entitlement not necessarily cash available to buy more shares?

4. Can this function safely identify already adjusted prices or missing terminal proceeds?

## Suggested answers

1. The holder now owns two shares: 2*50=100. The raw prices refer to different share units.

2. C=2 per opening share. Multiply the quoted 1 by the two ending shares once, then use that amount.

3. Payment may occur later. Today's receivable is valued at par; settlement and financing are not modeled.

4. No. Price basis must be verified upstream. Terminal-event evidence is separate, and zero/missing
endpoint prices are rejected rather than interpreted as a loss or recovery amount.
